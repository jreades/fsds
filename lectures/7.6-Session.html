Bunday (1963)

Just what constituted a natural break had never been precisely defined but they all felt that they recognised one when they saw it. ~ Bunday (1963, 1)

Margin note (p.1): this should sound familiar to anyone taking GIS: the natural breaks classification algorithm. But when is it the right algorithm to use?

The story is a faculty meeting with twelve students and eight papers. The staff total the raw marks: Condon is top (448) and Jolley bottom (357). Dr Caird suggests scaling each paper to 0–100: “Surely we should make the marks more comparable before we total them” (p.2; margin note: normalisation!). The scaled sheet is the raw order exactly reversed: Jolley top (429), Condon bottom (391). “This completely reverses the previous order” (p.3). Then they try ranks, and the third sheet is a tie: every student totals 52.

The quote is the first one in the paper. A “natural break” is what the classification chapter calls it: the same phrase is in the map classification video. The ending has a second one: the switch to ranks is agreed in time for “the arrival of coffee, the best natural break so far” (p.3).

Since the marks seem to lead us into confusion perhaps we should consider the rankings in each paper. ~ Bunday (1963, 3)

Margin notes (p.3): Professor Zangwell “was happy that the miracle rescue of Jolley had come to pass”, which is a nice bit of motivated reasoning. Think about the implications for data science: do we select representations that produce the answers we want? And the rankings are robust standardisation.

Reading notes: nobody asks what the marks measure. Each change is made to “be fair”. Say that this is the question for the group briefing: every step is defensible and the conclusion has moved.

Lu & Henning (2013)

A statistical population only exists in the theoretical world. ~ Lu and Henning (2013, 67)

Margin notes (p.67): the word “population” means different things to laypeople and statisticians. The key insight: separate the statistical population, the product of a data-generating process, from the physical one. Whether the model fits is a question of fit, not a casual explanation.

The 100-student example (p.67): if you sample the entire class, there’s no sampling error, so what are you looking at? Margin note: this connects to “big data and the end of theory”. What are you looking at when there’s no sample? The reframing (p.68): the aim is “not [to] generalize to a population but to make some empirically defensible statement about a learning method, which can only be described as a process”. Margin note: a much more careful framing than most articles or “big data” claims.

The paper is a teaching paper. The authors argue that a “population” is a mathematical idea, and that what we actually have is a data-generating process. The page that sets this out is p.67 with Figure 1: the real world on the left, the theoretical world on the right, and the data-generating process as the bridge.

It is never a matter of if the model is wrong (it is, as George Box assures us), but how wrong it is. ~ Lu and Henning (2013, 68)

Reading notes: pp.67–69 only: Figure 1, the problem with “population”, and the retail cashier example. A Normal distribution allows negative salaries, so “salaries are Normal” is false. The authors’ fix is to say “salaries are modelled using a normal distribution”. The change is, in their words, “minor semantically but profound conceptually” (p.68). Margin note: a really strong argument. It relies partly on being precise with terminology (“modelled using…”) and partly on the usefulness of the method, rather than presenting it as a reflection of the real world.

Link to the practical: when you plot a distribution this week, you are choosing a model of how the data were made. The same sentence applies to a map classification.

Arribas-Bel (2014)

[U]nlike a census or an economic survey, specifically created with research and policy analysis in mind, these sources were not originally intended for this end but for other purposes. ~ Arribas-Bel (2014, 45)

Margin notes (p.45): the data arrive as a side effect, not by design. The three features are accidental, open and everywhere, but “open” is no longer so simple (X shutting down its feed; Google’s terms for Street View imagery). Free access holds up to a point: firms provide feeds while it suits them and can withdraw them at any time (p.47: many APIs are now shut, or need NDAs or sign-off from a legal team). Government commitments are more binding, but open data doesn’t mean the data are maintained, or that the whole pipeline is open. Postcodes: the boundaries aren’t widely available, and even with academic access you can’t publish derived data on them because the PAF belongs to Royal Mail.

p.46: the three groups (people as sensors, web companies, open government data) are a useful classification, but not a clean one: they mix (e.g. “civic apps”). Citizens as sensors, where they are equipped and able to take part.

p.46: he says the major flaw “may relate to the quality of the data”. True, but it misses why the data were collected. The TfL example: the ticketing system is designed to price fares, not track people, but because fares depend loosely on distance we can mostly get entry and exit on the Tube. New York’s flat fare never needed to know where people left, so it never asked. The whole design reflects that difference.

p.50: nothing about the cost of maintaining the data.

The extent to which this is a problem depends of course on both the exact type and source of data as well as the particular question being analyzed. ~ Arribas-Bel (2014, 50)

Margin notes (p.50): this is the key point. Most of the old Twitter and Foursquare location research used those data because they were accessible, and took no account of who uses Twitter. The well-conceived exception is Hristova et al. on gentrification in East London: you would expect the gentrifying group to be over-represented on those platforms and to have location services switched on. Ahlfeldt (2013) on Flickr and house prices is another good example: the bias towards young professionals is the population of potential buyers.

If time allows (p.50): he says researchers need “some basic general programming skills”. Is this still true now that there’s AI? Jon’s view: yes, because how would you know what an API is, or how to ask AI to use one?

Link to Question 1: Inside Airbnb is a collection of what hosts and guests wrote and did for other reasons. Ask what it can and can’t show about “out of control”, which is the “particular question being analyzed”.

3. How Much Is 250?

from shapely.geometry import Point

# a circle of radius 10 degrees, in EPSG:4326 (lat/long)
circle = Point(0, 0).buffer(10)
world = gpd.GeoSeries([circle], crs='EPSG:4326')
print(world.simplify(250).iloc[0].geom_type)
print(len(world.simplify(250).iloc[0].exterior.coords))

The circle has 65 coordinates. What does it print?

  1. Polygon, then 65
  2. Polygon, then about 30
  3. Polygon, then 4
  4. Polygon, then 0

Answer: 3. Polygon, then 4: a triangle.

The expected wrong answer is 2 or 1 (people think 250 is metres). Count hands.

Say: simplify works in the units of the coordinate reference system. Here those are degrees, so 250 degrees is far bigger than the whole circle and the shape collapses to a triangle. The code ran without an error and the answer was wrong. Nothing in the output tells you.

Case: Permitted Development

Offices into Homes

  • Since 2013, Permitted Development Rights (PDR) let developers convert offices into homes without full planning permission.
  • Before April 2021, there was no requirement to meet the national space standard (37 m² for a one-bedroom flat).
  • So: how small are these homes? And how would you find out across all of London?

Clock: 65:00. The case runs 65–85: about 4 minutes a slide, most of it in the pairs questions on “Linking” and “The Response”.

Sources: Chng, Reades, and Hubbard (2024, 961, 963–64). The Ilford example (p.963): single-aspect studio flats of 13 m² in an office block next to the A12.

Moved from week 6. Link back: last week’s atlas used the same EPC + Price Paid linkage; this is what happens when that kind of work is challenged in print.

Say: this is my own work, with Ian Chng and Phil Hubbard, and it was then challenged in print. I’m going to show you both sides, and you’re going to put both on the frame.

Linking Three Data Sets

Frame What we did
Inputs London Planning Database: 2,323 PDR schemes, 2013–2021, 18,000+ homes
Cleaning Match each scheme to Energy Performance Certificates (floor area) and Price Paid Data (price) by address
Method Compare floor areas with the space standard

Every match is an address problem: a reused property ID, a building that’s gone but still in the postal data, a postcode that changes.

Source: Chng, Reades, and Hubbard (2024, 963).

This is where the address-matching examples from week 5 belong (all in the cleaning box): one UPRN entered 20–50 times on EPCs for a new development because the new flats didn’t have their own yet; a building destroyed and rebuilt that the postal data never records; a large development that gets a new postcode. Every failed match is a home that drops out of the analysis without anyone deciding to drop it.

Ask (pairs, 1 min): what kind of home is most likely to fail to match?

What We Found

Figure 8 shows large PDR developments (33.7%) have delivered a greater proportion of units smaller than the NDSS minimum for a one-bedroom home compared to small PDR developments (14.1%) […] the values here are significantly lower than the 86.4% Clifford et al. (2019) and 70% Ferm et al. (2021) identified in their studies ~ Chng, Reades, and Hubbard (2024, 973)

Same question. Very different numbers. Who’s right?

Note what the paper says in the same paragraph: it used 35 m², not 37 m², as the threshold because EPC floor areas are often wrong (“as many as one-in-four EPCs report floor space that varies by 10% or more”, p.973), and it says some larger flats may also be below the standard for their size. The caveats were in the paper.

Don’t answer “who’s right” yet: the next slide is the response.

The Response

EPCs are about energy performance, not space standards albeit they do include floorspace data ~ Clifford et al. (2025, 674)

The EPC data does not show the number of bedrooms, and so Chng et al. (2024) have just applied the minimum standard for a one bedroom, one person residence to everything they have analysed. ~ Clifford et al. (2025, 677)

Their three issues: complexity, accuracy, availability. Which box does each one go in?

Pairs, 2 minutes: place the three issues on the frame. Likely answers: complexity (the missing bedroom count; one standard applied to everything) → inputs and method; accuracy (EPC floor areas are estimates) → inputs; availability (what data exists at all) → inputs, but also analyst (who can collect it).

Say the “no right of reply” part here, briefly and in your own words. It’s part of the lesson: this is how these debates unfold in print, and not always fairly. Several of the authors are UCL colleagues.

Where Is the ‘Nothing’?

  • The EPC has no bedroom field. So one rule was applied to every home.
  • Their suggested fix: the EPC does record habitable rooms, so assume bedrooms = rooms − 1 (Clifford et al. 2025, 677).
  • Their worked example: studio flats of 21–28 m² measured from floorplans, 20–33 m² in the EPCs (Clifford et al. 2025, 675).

A city-wide linkage vs 30 schemes measured by hand: what does each one give up?

This is the week’s question in a real dispute: a missing field decided a result. And the fix was in another column, which is the case for reading the data’s documentation before you decide what’s missing.

The contrast is the same as Ollie’s measurement vs memory in week 5. The big-data approach gives up detail (bedrooms, floorplans) to get coverage (all 33 boroughs). The case-study approach gives up coverage to get detail: 30 schemes, chosen in places with lots of PDR. Neither is the view from nowhere. Ask: which box on the frame does each approach strengthen, and which does it weaken?

If someone asks for the verdict: both studies find a real problem with PDR housing. They disagree about how big it is, and that disagreement is about the data.

Where Did This Number Come From?

Crime Figures Count Reports

Raw crime rates are used to find hotspots. But:

  • not every victim reports a crime, and the ones who do aren’t a random sample;
  • pickpocketing and assault are both “crime”;
  • the rate depends on whether you count residents or the people who are there;
  • the law and policing differ between places and months.

So, is Kensington and Chelsea more dangerous than Peckham?

Clock: 85:00. Four slides, about 5 minutes each; finish by 105:00.

Say: the data-generating process is not the crime. It’s the report, the police’s decision to record it, and the law. Whoever built the data decided what counts.

Ask pairs (1 minute of silent writing first): “More dangerous for whom?” Two pairs.

When Reports Go Up

Many of the colleges with higher reported rates of sexual assault were actually places where more institutional resources were being devoted to support for survivors. D’Ignazio and Klein (2020, 10)

So colleges with higher reported assault rates were often the ones putting more resources into supporting survivors, and colleges have a financial incentive not to report. A safer-looking campus may be one that is counting less.

This is also a good illustration of why geospatial analyses of crime data are nearly always problematic: reported crime is not the same as actual crime. Who reports and why varies enormously. As well, not all crimes are equal: we surely care more about murder than pickpocketing, but I have seen analyses that treated these as ‘just crime’.

So, Can You Prove Anything with Numbers?
