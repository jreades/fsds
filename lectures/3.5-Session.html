This Week’s Question

When you decide how to store data, are you just organising it, or already deciding what it means?

 

We’ll answer it twice: with Python, with cities.

Asking a group worked for diversifying feedback. So don’t ask for volunteers: walk to a row and ask “What did you two decide?” The answer is then “we”, not “I”.

In the DOLs video I say there’s “no right way” to store data, then, thirty seconds later, that there is a right answer. Today is about which of those is true.

1. Deliberately Similar

cities = [
  'San Francisco',
  'London',
  'Paris',
  'Beijing']

# Prints London
print(cities[2])
  1. San Francisco
  2. London
  3. Paris
  4. IndexError

Answer: 3. Indexes start at 0. And the comment is wrong: it’s copied from my own slide in Dictionaries.

From Dictionaries (“Deliberately Similar”). The point: a comment is context, and context can be wrong. Remember that for the readings.

Story (≤ 60 seconds): why a dictionary key has to be immutable using example of zebras relabelled as finches.

2. Find the 5

my_list = [
  [1, 2, 3],
  [4, 5, 6],
  [7, 8, 9]
]

Which prints 5?

  1. my_list[1][1]
  2. my_list[2][2]
  3. my_list[1,1]
  4. my_list[4]

Answer: 1. Row 1, column 1, counting from zero. (2 gives 9; 3 is a TypeError; 4 is an IndexError.)

From LOLs. Story if time: a list-of-lists is like the reference list in a journal article. It points to other things rather than containing them.

But what does my_list actually look like if you take a step back and squint? Answer: a matrix or a data set!

D’Ignazio & Klein (2020)

Although GDELT de-duplicated some of those stories to a single event, it still logged, erroneously, that hundreds of kidnapping events had happened that day. ~ D’Ignazio and Klein (2020, 2)

[…] data enter into research projects already fully cooked—the result of a complex set of social, political, and historical circumstances. ~ D’Ignazio and Klein (2020, 13)

The first quote is about FiveThirtyEight’s use of GDELT after the Chibok kidnapping: media reports were counted as events.

“Fully cooked” is D’Ignazio and Klein drawing on Gitelman and Jackson, whose line is “‘Raw data’ is an oxymoron”.

Same Diagnosis, Different Cure?

Compare the perspectives of:

But deep context and computation are not incompatible. ~ D’Ignazio and Klein (2020, 16)

And:

City-making is always, simultaneously, an enactment of city-knowing — which cannot be reduced to computation. ~ Mattern (2017)

On the week’s question they’re on the same side: both would vote 3. The difference is in the ‘cure’, not the diagnosis.

The plenary’s opening question: “Where would each of them send you to find the missing context?”

  • D’Ignazio and Klein: inside data science. Go back to whoever collected the data, the domain experts, and the data’s history (the data biography). Computation done better.
  • Mattern: outside the database. Librarians, archives, residents, maintenance, and knowledge from the senses and long familiarity with a place. She questions the ‘city as computer’ frame itself, though she doesn’t reject data (“and, yes, by systematically filtering data”).

Don’t invent a fight: this is a difference of emphasis. Indeed, I think Catherine and Lauren would actually agree with Shannon on this, but in this particular chapter they put the emphasis in different places. Indeed, D’Ignazio and Klein cite Mattern on librarians being left out of smart-city conversations (p.26).

My margin note: “Why these are paired.”
