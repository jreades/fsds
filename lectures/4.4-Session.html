How I store data is a neutral choice, because storage itself is just a technical tool.

Last week’s stem was “How I store data is / isn’t a neutral choice, because…”. There were 43 responses (up from 17 in week 2). Almost all said it isn’t neutral; only one said it is (the third quote). A few answered on practical grounds (security, privacy, “easier to browse”) without taking a side.

The most common reason was personal bias or lived experience (“I already have some sort of bias”). Next came access and power: who can see it, open vs proprietary formats. Fewer said that the categories themselves decide what exists, which is the point of the first quote.

Most of you wrote “I have bias” as if it were a flaw. D’Ignazio & Klein turn that round: they disclose their positions and treat them as “a set of valuable perspectives that could frame our work” (p.83). Come back to this on the D’Ignazio & Klein slide.

Say: these are anonymous, they get read, and three of them open each session. There’s one at the end of today too.

Why these three?

  • “What to leave out”: a function or a package also decides what you see and what gets wrapped away.
  • “Where, how or why”: that’s what you need to know before you trust someone else’s data, or their code.
  • “Just a technical tool”: true in a narrow sense, if the only question is whether data can be written and read back. But who can read it back depends on the format.

Handle the third quote as a fair, narrowly technical answer, not a wrong one. Then widen it: “read back” assumes you can open the file. XLS, SPSS .sav files and ESRI’s File Geodatabase were closed formats. You needed a paid-for application to open them, unless someone reverse-engineered the format (that’s how LibreOffice, PSPP and GDAL read them). Even the Shapefile, whose specification ESRI published, is still a format one company controls. So even the technical choice decides who gets access.

D’Ignazio & Klein have a line for this: “neither graphic is neutral, but both graphics are factual” (p.80). A format can be technically correct and still not neutral.

The videos make the same “just a tool” case for functions and packages. Today we test it.

Today: storing data was a choice. Is reusing other people’s code, and other people’s views, one too?

1. Why Haven’t These Changed?

data    = [1,25,-4,14,7,9]
total   = 1
numbers = []

def calc_mean(numbers):
  total = 0.0
  count = len(numbers)
  for i in numbers:
    total += i
  return total/count

print(calc_mean(data))
print(total)

What does the last line print?

  1. 1
  2. 0.0
  3. 52.0
  4. NameError

Answer: 1. The total inside the function is a different variable. (And numbers is still [].)

From Functions (“But Notice!”). The function can’t see out, and the rest of the code can’t see in.

Story: the chart whose top gets chopped off once orders pass 1,000. Copy-pasted into ten places, it’s ten fixes; in a function, it’s one.

2. Wrapping Paper

def splitter(func):
  def wrapper():
    return func().split()
  return wrapper

@splitter
@better
def hello():
  return('hello world')

print(hello())

(better adds title case and !.)

  1. Hello World!
  2. ['Hello', 'World!']
  3. ['hello', 'world']
  4. AttributeError

Answer: 2. better runs first, then splitter. Swap the two lines and you get AttributeError: 'list' object has no attribute 'title'.

From Decorators (“Chaining Decorators”). Jon’s image: wrapping paper. The layer furthest from the function is the outer layer, and order matters.

If asked: .title() works on strings, but after splitter you have a list.

3. Gotcha!

pi = 3.1415
print(pi) # 3.1415

from math import pi
print(pi)
  1. 3.1415
  2. 3.141592653589793
  3. NameError
  4. Both values

Answer: 2. Your pi has been replaced, with no warning.

From Packages (“Gotcha!”, Approach 2). Jon calls this “copy-pasting in its most extreme form”. With import math as m, both values would coexist in different namespaces.

4. Who’s Calling?

def better(func):
  '''Better formatting of a string'''
  def wrapper():
    '''Wraps a function to format it.'''
    return func().title() + "!"
  return wrapper

@better
def hello():
  '''Prints hello world'''
  return('hello world')

print(hello.__name__)
  1. hello
  2. wrapper
  3. better
  4. None

Answer: 2. And hello.__doc__ is the wrapper’s docstring.

From Decorators (“Making Use of Metadata”). Count the hands for 1 (hello): that’s how many people expected the function to keep its own identity.

Massey (1996)

[…] the boundaries which we draw in space, the ‘places’ we define […], the decisions about which mobilities to allow and which not, and about how open, or how closed, our places are to be … all these things, rather than being based on some eternal principles, are in fact expressions of, and exercises in, social power. ~ Massey (1996, 117)

Reading notes:

  • Massey’s ‘geographical imaginations’: the global (‘the world is flat’, the “annihilation of space”) and the defensible local. People with power often hold both at once: free movement of capital, but not of people.
  • The difference between Chiapas holding on to its ‘local’ and California holding on to its ‘local’ is the power geometry.

If a pair is stuck, ask: “Who gets to draw the boundary?” Or use Massey’s own pair: she sympathises with the Native American chief defending his land but opposes Le Pen, though both are ‘defending the local’ (p.117). The difference is the power geometry.

For students new to social science theory, this is problematisation: if there’s no general rule for when a boundary is right, then the boundary is a social construct.

[…] although these workplaces are globalised (if only very narrowly so) and in that sense ‘open’, they are also specialised and excluding places, closed and defensive, quite tightly sealed against ‘non-conforming’ invasions from the outside world. ~ Massey (1996, 121)

The high-tech scientists: workplaces sealed for “high-level thought”, with security guards told to keep children out at weekends, while the home is “muddled, multiple, even open and porous”.

Link to the videos: Functions says “Your function should never know anything about the surrounding context […] and the code that calls the function should never know anything about what happens inside the function.” That’s good engineering, and it’s the same move as the science park.

D’Ignazio & Klein (2020)

The view from nowhere–from a distance, from up above, like a god–may be data visualization’s most signature feature. It’s also the most ethically complicated to navigate for the ways in which it masks the people, the methods, the questions, and the messiness that lies behind clean lines and geometric shapes. ~ D’Ignazio and Klein (2020, 76)

“The god trick of seeing everything from nowhere” is Donna Haraway’s phrase. D’Ignazio and Klein quote it on the same page.

The Porter quote on the question slide is from this page too. They also note that in most “neutral” visualisations, “this perspective is the one of the dominant, default group” (p.76): my view is ‘natural’, so everyone else is pushing an agenda.

Reading notes: “The more plain, the more neutral; the more neutral, the more objective; and the more objective, the more true—or so this line of reasoning goes” (p.76). The four conventions that make a chart look factual (p.82): two-dimensional viewpoints, clean layouts, geometric shapes and lines, and a data source at the bottom.

[…] all forms of knowledge […] are situated, [Haraway] explains, meaning that they are produced by specific people in specific circumstances—cultural, historical, and geographic. ~ D’Ignazio and Klein (2020, 83)

“She” is Haraway again. Situated knowledge is the alternative to the view from nowhere: say where you are standing. This is the answer to the exit tickets that said “I have bias”: state your positionality, and the bias becomes a frame (p.83). Also: “When visualizing data, the only certifiable fact is that it’s impossible to avoid interpretation” (p.80).

Aside, if there’s time: “Fields very close to visualization, like cartography, have long seen their work as ideological” (p.80). On 4 September 2026, the UN General Assembly voted to replace the traditional (Mercator) world map with one that more accurately reflects the size of Africa (BBC). The “Correct the Map” resolution was sponsored by Togo, backed by African Union members, and passed 164 to 1, with six abstentions. The US was the only vote against. It’s non-binding. There are also allegations that Trump’s interest in Greenland is partly driven by how large it looks on Mercator maps.

Is Massey Against Closed Places?

Even as I felt critical of the scientists’ exclusive workplaces I remembered Virginia Woolf’s call for a room of her own. ~ Massey (1996, 123)

A function is a room of its own. Is that a problem?

Massey doesn’t say open is good and closed is bad. She says there are “few – if any – abstract, generalisable, ‘rules’” (p.117), and what matters is who has the power to open or close a place. Openness can leave “the less powerful places […] open to indiscriminate invasion and disruption” (p.123).

D’Ignazio & Klein end in the same place: rather than “universal rules and ratios (think: data-ink)”, work toward “a more holistic and more inclusive ideal” (p.96). Neither reading gives you a rule.

So, closure (encapsulation) isn’t the problem. The question is who gets to decide what’s inside and what’s left out. That’s the bridge.

What’s Been Wrapped Away?

Hello, wrapper

Without @wraps:

print(hello.__name__)
# wrapper
print(hello.__doc__)
# Wraps a function to format it.

With @wraps(func):

print(hello.__name__)
# hello
print(hello.__doc__)
# Prints hello world

The frame replaced the name of whoever did the work. @wraps puts the provenance back.

Back to Q4. The output looked fine, but the function couldn’t say what it was any more. That’s D’Ignazio & Klein’s god trick in miniature: it “masks the people, the methods”.

Ask the room: “When you import a package, what has been wrapped away from you?” Take two answers from pairs.

Don’t Never Do a God Trick

Simply stated: it is never a good idea to say “never” in design. ~ D’Ignazio and Klein (2020, 91)

[…] there are also good reasons to use the god trick as a form of recuperation, contestation, or empowerment. ~ D’Ignazio and Klein (2020, 92)

Listen for: where does Ollie’s work come down to the ground?

Their example is Margaret Pearce’s Coming Home to Indigenous Place Names in Canada. It uses the look of the official national map, but is filled with place names collected from First Nations, Métis and Inuit peoples over fifteen months (pp.92–93). The god’s-eye view is turned against the coloniser’s view. It works within the norms of the prevailing power geometry (same paper size, fold, scale and projection as the official map), but subverts them.

Pearce also withheld data: she respected communities that didn’t want to share English translations, and protected “the exact geographic location of each site from being shared with outsiders” (p.92). That’s Massey’s Woolf point: closure chosen by the less powerful. And it’s week 3 again: who gets access to what you store?

Handoff: satellite imagery is the ultimate view from above. Ollie’s work with Bellingcat checks it against evidence from the ground. Ask students to listen for that, because the exit ticket asks about it.
